How Protected Lanes Reshaped a Mid-Sized City's Commute

By Fixture Author · 1 October 2026

Protected lanes change who feels safe enough to ride. Three years after the city finished its first connected network of physically separated bike lanes, the clearest shift is not in the traffic counts but in the faces on the road: more parents with children, more older riders, and more people who say they had never considered cycling before.

The network covers just under forty kilometres and links the main railway station, two hospitals, the university district and the old market quarter. Planners chose routes that already carried heavy foot and vehicle traffic, reasoning that a lane nobody can reach is a lane nobody will use. Each segment is separated from moving cars by a kerb, a row of planters or a line of parked vehicles.

What the counts show

Automatic counters installed on eleven corridors recorded a rise of roughly sixty percent in weekday bicycle trips between the year before construction and the most recent autumn. Weekend use grew faster still, which officials read as a sign that recreational and family riders were joining commuters. Motor traffic on the same corridors fell by a smaller margin, and the city's own survey suggests that many of the missing car trips were short ones that had simply moved to two wheels.

Journey times tell a similar story. Over distances under five kilometres, riders on the new network now report door-to-door times that match or beat those of drivers at peak hours, largely because they skip the search for parking. Bus operators, who feared the lanes would slow their routes, have reported little change, although two junctions needed signal retiming after the first winter.

Not every number moved in the right direction. Minor collisions between riders and people stepping out of parked vehicles rose slightly in the first year, before a campaign and a redesign of the parking buffer brought them back down. The city says it now treats door-zone conflicts as a design problem rather than a behavioural one.

Money, politics and patience

The programme cost about as much as resurfacing six kilometres of arterial road. Supporters point out that maintenance is cheaper, since lighter vehicles wear a surface far more slowly, and that the network was built in stages so that each phase could be judged on its results before the next was funded.

Opposition was loud at the start. Shopkeepers on the market streets feared losing customers who arrived by car. Retail turnover on those streets has since edged upward, and a merchants' association that once petitioned against the scheme now asks for more bicycle parking outside its doors. Local officials caution that the data cannot prove cause, since the district also gained a new tram stop during the same period.

What the project does show, say the engineers involved, is the value of continuity. Early plans offered isolated stretches of lane that ended abruptly at busy junctions. Riders avoided them. Only when the stretches were joined into a route that could be followed from end to end did the numbers climb, and they climbed quickly.

Looking ahead, the transport department intends to extend the network into two suburban districts and to add secure parking at the railway station. It is also testing seasonal maintenance schedules, because a lane buried under snow or leaves is a lane that sends riders back into traffic. Early trials with dedicated sweeping and clearing crews suggest that winter use can be sustained if the surface is treated as seriously as the road beside it.

For now, residents seem to have made up their minds. In the latest survey, a clear majority said they supported keeping the lanes, including many who never ride. As one planner put it, the best evidence is the line of bicycles chained up outside the station on a wet Tuesday morning.
